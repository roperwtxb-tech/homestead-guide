Preserving the Harvest

The one rule that matters most

Low-acid foods — vegetables, meat, poultry, seafood, and any mixture of these (soups, stews) — must be processed in a pressure canner. A boiling water bath never gets hot enough to kill Clostridium botulinum spores, the bacteria that causes botulism. Botulism toxin can be fatal and often can't be detected by smell, taste, or appearance.

High-acid foods — fruit, jam/jelly, and properly acidified pickles/salsa/tomatoes — can be safely processed in a boiling water bath because the acid prevents botulism spores from growing.

Tomatoes are borderline. Modern tomato varieties are often lower-acid than older ones. Always add bottled lemon juice (2 tbsp/quart or 1 tbsp/pint) or citric acid (¼ tsp/quart) to canned tomatoes and tomato products, even if the recipe doesn't call for it.

Water Bath Canning — the basics

  1. Inspect jars for chips/cracks; wash jars, lids, and bands in hot soapy water.
  2. Prepare food and pack into jars (raw pack or hot pack), leaving the headspace the recipe specifies (usually ¼"–1").
  3. Remove air bubbles, wipe rims clean, center lid, screw band to "fingertip tight."
  4. Lower jars into a canner of simmering water; water should cover jars by 1–2 inches.
  5. Bring to a rolling boil, start timing, and process for the full time in a tested recipe.
  6. Turn off heat, remove lid, let jars sit 5 minutes, then remove to a towel-lined counter.
  7. Don't touch lids for 12–24 hrs. Check seals (lid should not flex when pressed), label, and store.

Pressure Canning — the basics

  1. Add the amount of water the canner manufacturer specifies (usually 2–3 inches).
  2. Load prepared jars onto the rack, lock the lid.
  3. Vent steam for 10 minutes before pressurizing — this step is not optional, it removes trapped air that causes inaccurate pressure.
  4. Bring to the pressure the recipe (and your altitude) requires, and start timing once pressure is reached.
  5. Maintain steady pressure for the full processing time, adjusting heat as needed.
  6. Turn off heat and let pressure drop to zero naturally — never force-cool or quick-release a canner.
  7. Wait 10 minutes after pressure hits zero, then unlock and remove the lid away from your face.

🟡 Moderate histamine Canning's heat processing itself doesn't produce histamine the way fermenting does, but two things in this tab commonly show up on low-histamine lists anyway: vinegar-forward pickles/relish/salsa (vinegar is a histamine-liberating food for some people), and canned fish or meat, which — like any shelf-stable protein — has had time to accumulate histamine by the time it's opened. Tomatoes specifically are also flagged as histamine-liberating on their own, independent of how they're preserved. Fresh or freshly frozen versions of the same foods are the lower-histamine choice.
🌾 Gluten swap Plain distilled white vinegar and cider vinegar (what most tested canning recipes call for) are gluten-free even though distilled from grain — the distillation process leaves the gluten behind. Just watch for malt vinegar specifically (made from barley, not distilled the same way) in any pickle or chutney recipe, and swap in distilled white or cider vinegar instead.

Dehydrating basics

Slice food uniformly so pieces dry at the same rate. Pretreat most fruit with a quick dip in an ascorbic acid/lemon-juice solution to stop browning, and blanch most vegetables briefly in boiling water or steam before drying — it slows nutrient loss and keeps color and texture better in storage.

After drying, condition fruits and vegetables: let the batch cool, then seal loosely in a jar for 4–10 days, shaking daily. If moisture beads on the glass, redry the batch — uneven moisture is the #1 cause of mold in stored dried food.

Store fully cooled, fully dry food in airtight containers (jars, mylar bags with oxygen absorbers) in a cool, dark place. Most dried fruits and vegetables keep 6–12 months at room temperature, or 1–2 years in the freezer; jerky keeps 1–2 months at room temp, up to a year frozen.

Meat and jerky are the exception to "just dry it." Dehydrators often don't reach a high enough temperature to kill bacteria as food dries. USDA guidance: heat meat to 160°F (beef) or poultry to 165°F — either by simmering marinated strips first, or by heating dried jerky in a 275°F oven for 10 minutes after dehydrating — before storing it at room temperature.
🔴 Histamine Jerky and other slow-dried meats are commonly flagged as high-histamine — the long, warm drying time allows the same slow protein breakdown that makes cured meat high-histamine. 🟡 Moderate Dried fruit shows up on many low-histamine lists too, though tolerance varies more person to person than with dried meat — fresh or frozen fruit is the safer default if you're watching this closely.
🌾 Gluten swap If you're dehydrating cooked grains or pasta for backpacking-style meals, use a GF grain (rice, quinoa) or GF pasta — the drying process itself doesn't add gluten, but the grain you start with does if it's wheat-based.

Freezing basics

Most vegetables need a quick blanch (boil, then ice-water bath) before freezing — it stops the enzyme action that would otherwise turn them mushy and dull in the freezer. Fruit generally doesn't need blanching; a quick open-freeze on a tray before bagging keeps pieces separate. Pack out as much air as possible to slow freezer burn, label with the date, and use the oldest items first.

🔴 Histamine Every fermented food is a high-histamine food — the same bacteria that makes fermentation safe and delicious also produces histamine as it works. This is true of sauerkraut, kimchi, and any lacto-ferment no matter how carefully it's made. Set aside a fresh, unfermented portion for the low-histamine plate before it goes into the ferment crock.

Lacto-fermentation basics

Salt does the safety work here: it favors the lactic-acid bacteria that naturally occur on vegetables while suppressing the bacteria that cause spoilage. Two common approaches:

  • Dry-salt method (shredded veg — kraut, kimchi): salt at roughly 2% of the vegetable's weight (about 1½–2 tbsp fine salt per 5 lbs of shredded cabbage), then massage/pound until it releases enough brine to submerge itself.
  • Brine method (whole/cut veg — pickles, whole cabbage leaves): mix a brine of 2–5% salt by weight of the water (roughly 1½–3½ tbsp non-iodized salt per quart of water), and submerge the vegetables completely.

Keep everything under the brine with a weight, cover loosely (gas needs to escape), and ferment at room temperature (60–75°F is ideal) out of direct sun. Taste starting around day 3–5; it's done when it tastes sour enough for you — then move it to the fridge to slow fermentation.

Normal & expected

  • Bubbling, fizzing, or a hiss when you burp the jar
  • Cloudy brine and some sediment on the bottom
  • A thin, white, harmless film (kahm yeast) — skim it off, ferment is still fine
  • Sour, tangy, pleasantly funky smell
  • Color dulling slightly (kraut going from bright to olive-green is normal)

Spoiled — throw it out

  • Fuzzy mold in any color other than white kahm film — pink, black, green, blue
  • Sliminess on the vegetables themselves (not just the brine)
  • A putrid, rotten, or otherwise off smell instead of sour/tangy
  • Vegetables floating above the brine and visibly rotting
  • Any pink or orange discoloration in the brine itself
Starter recipes: classic sauerkraut is just cabbage + 2% salt. Kimchi adds garlic, ginger, scallion, and gochugaru to napa cabbage in the same salt range. Fridge dill pickles use a 3.5% brine with dill, garlic, and a splash of vinegar for extra tang and crunch.
🌾 Gluten swap If a kimchi recipe calls for gochujang paste, check the label — many brands include wheat flour; several brands are labeled gluten-free. Fish sauce is usually gluten-free but brands vary, so check that label too. Plain gochugaru (Korean chili flakes) is naturally gluten-free.
🔴 Histamine Cured, smoked, and aged meats are some of the highest-histamine foods there are — bacon, salami, cured ham, and smoked sausage all get their flavor from the same slow bacterial/enzymatic process that steadily raises histamine. Freshly cooked, unaged meat (see the Freezing tab) is the low-histamine alternative for the same cuts.

Curing salts — what they're for

Cure #1 (often sold as "Prague Powder #1" or "Insta Cure #1") is a mix of table salt and 6.25% sodium nitrite, dyed pink so it's never mistaken for regular salt. It's used for short-cure items you'll cook and eat soon: bacon, corned beef, sausage, ham, pastrami. Cure #2 adds sodium nitrate as well, which slowly converts to nitrite over weeks — it's for long, air-dried products like salami and prosciutto that cure for weeks or months without cooking.

This is the one ingredient in this whole guide where a mistake can be lethal. Regulations cap sodium nitrite at 200 ppm in finished meat; most home recipes target well under that. A commonly published commercial rate is about 1 level teaspoon (≈6g) of Cure #1 per 5 lbs of meat for dry cures — but brands vary in density, so always weigh on a gram scale and follow the exact instructions printed on your specific package rather than any rate here. Store it away from children, labeled clearly, and never substitute it teaspoon-for-teaspoon with table salt in any other recipe.

Hot smoking (recommended default)

Hot smoking cooks the food while it smokes, at smoker temperatures of 225–300°F. This is the safe, forgiving method for home use because the meat reaches a fully-cooked internal temperature:

FoodSafe internal temp
Whole cuts (steak, chops, roasts)145°F + 3 min rest
Ground meat160°F
Poultry (whole or parts)165°F

Use two thermometers — one for the smoker's air temperature, one probe for the meat itself. Always thaw meat completely first; a still-frozen center spends too long in the 40–140°F "danger zone."

Cold smoking (advanced, higher risk)

Cold smoking flavors food at low temperatures (under ~90°F) without cooking it. Because the food never reaches a safe temperature, cold-smoked meat and fish must be cured first (see Cure #1/#2 above) to control bacterial growth during the long smoke. Cold smoking is best reserved for already food-safe items like hard cheese, or for cured products, and is not a good starting point for beginners — the margin for error is much smaller than hot smoking.

Jerky, specifically

See the Dehydrating tab for full temp/time charts. The safety step that matters most: bring marinated strips to a full simmer (or heat dried jerky in a 275°F oven for 10 minutes) so the meat actually reaches 160°F (beef) / 165°F (poultry) at some point in the process — drying alone at typical dehydrator temperatures does not reliably kill bacteria.

🌾 Gluten swap Brines and marinades are where gluten sneaks into cured/smoked meat: soy sauce and Worcestershire sauce are traditionally wheat-based (swap in tamari, coconut aminos, or a GF-labeled Worcestershire), beer brines use regular beer (swap in a certified GF beer, cider, or broth), and homemade sausage sometimes uses bread-based "rusk" as a filler (swap in rice, GF breadcrumbs, or skip it).

Root cellaring basics

Most root cellar crops want one of two conditions: cold & moist (32–40°F, 85–95% humidity — think a damp basement corner or a buried box) or cool & dry (50–60°F, 60–70% humidity — a garage shelf or dry closet). Mixing the two groups shortens storage life for both, and one rotting item can spoil a whole bin — check stored produce every couple of weeks and pull anything soft.

Butter, from the milk log

Skim cream off fresh milk (or use heavy cream), let it come to about 55–60°F, and shake or churn until it separates into butter solids and buttermilk — usually 10–20 minutes by hand mixer. Pour off the buttermilk (save it for baking), then "wash" the butter under cold water, kneading until the water runs clear — this removes residual buttermilk that would otherwise sour the butter. Salt to taste if desired, and store cold.

Basic soft cheese (chevre-style)

Warm milk to about 86°F, add a mesophilic culture (or a splash of cultured buttermilk) and a few drops of rennet, and let it sit undisturbed at room temperature for 12–24 hours until it sets into a solid curd. Ladle into a cloth-lined colander and let it drain 6–12 hours (longer = firmer cheese), then salt and refrigerate. It typically keeps 1–2 weeks in the fridge.

Raw milk note: if you're using unpasteurized milk, keep it well chilled, use it quickly, and know that soft/fresh cheeses make it easiest for any bacteria present to survive and multiply — aged hard cheeses are generally considered lower risk than fresh soft ones.
🔴 Histamine Aged and hard cheeses are some of the most concentrated histamine sources in any diet — the longer a cheese ages, the more histamine it accumulates. Fresh butter and quick, unaged soft cheese (like the chevre above, eaten within a few days) are much lower, though still cultured, so they're worth watching individually rather than assuming they're histamine-free.

Succession planting

For crops you eat continuously rather than all at once — lettuce, radishes, bush beans, carrots, cilantro, spinach — sow a short new row every 2–3 weeks through the growing season instead of all at once. Stop new sowings early enough that the crop's days to harvest still fits before the end of its planting window.

🧪 Before you plant

Test the soil first. Every 2–3 years, send a sample to Texas A&M AgriLife's Soil, Water and Forage Testing Lab in College Station. The Callahan County Extension office has sample bags and forms. The results tell you your pH and exactly what to add, so you're not guessing.

Expect alkaline soil. Callahan County soils are mostly loam over clay, neutral to moderately alkaline. High pH locks up iron: the newest leaves turn yellow while their veins stay green (iron chlorosis). The fix is chelated iron labeled EDDHA, which keeps working at high pH. A foliar iron spray is a quick but short-lived fix.

Fertilizer. For clay soils AgriLife suggests 1–2 lb of 10-20-10 (or 12-24-12) per 100 sq ft before planting. Your soil test results override this.

Water and mulch. Aim for about 1 inch a week, soaking at least 6 inches deep. AgriLife calls drip irrigation the best option: it keeps leaves dry (less disease) and uses the least water. Mulch 1–2 inches in general and 4–6 inches around tomatoes in summer to keep roots cool.

Wind. Wrap new tomato cages with row cover or perforated plastic right after transplanting. A windbreak row of sorghum, sunflowers or rye shelters about 10 ft downwind for every foot of its height.

About your frost dates. This guide uses April 5 and November 1 from the Baird and Clyde weather stations, the closest to you. Abilene's averages are a bit milder (about March 21 and November 11). Sticking with the more conservative dates protects you from a late cold snap.

🍅 Tomatoes, peppers & eggplant in Texas heat

Heat is the real enemy, not cold. Tomatoes stop setting fruit when nights stay above about 70–75°F or days run above about 95°F. Plant transplants as early as frost allows (mid-April for you) so the plants set fruit before late-June heat shuts them down.

Tomato varieties that handle Texas: Texas Superstar picks Tomato444 (BHN 444) and Tycoon, plus Celebrity, Carnival, Sun Pride and Heatwave for heat set. Cherry types (Porter, Juliet, Cherry Grande, Sweet 100) keep producing longest. Roma for paste.

Peppers: Big Bertha and Jupiter (bell), TAM Mild Jalapeño, Hidalgo Serrano. Eggplant: Black Beauty, Black Magic. Both shrug off summer better than tomatoes: keep them watered and they'll produce again in fall.

The fall crop. Texas grows a real second tomato season. Transplant in early to late July, about 100–120 days before first frost; AgriLife's last good date for your region is around July 25. Peppers and eggplant go in mid to late July. Use transplants, not seed, and give them afternoon shade and plenty of water their first couple of weeks. Tycoon resists tomato yellow leaf curl virus, which shows up more in fall.

Spacing: tomatoes 3–4 ft apart in rows 3–4 ft apart; peppers and eggplant 1½–2 ft apart.

🥦 Cool-season crops: fall is your better season

Broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, lettuce, spinach, kale, collards, chard, carrots and beets all grow in spring too, but here spring plantings race into heat and bolt or turn bitter. Broccoli, for example, flowers early once it matures much above 80°F. AgriLife says fall planting is often more successful in Texas, and doesn't recommend spring cauliflower at all.

Fall timing for you: broccoli, cabbage and cauliflower transplants mid-July through late August; lettuce late July through August; spinach, turnips, radishes and English peas keep going into October. The Planting Calendar's fall table has exact windows. Cold-hardy crops handle light freezes and keep producing past first frost; kale and collards taste sweeter after one.

Spring timing: transplants go in about 4–6 weeks before last frost (late February to early March for you). Pick early-maturing varieties.

Varieties (AgriLife North Central Texas list): broccoli Packman, Green Magic, Premium Crop; cabbage Early Jersey Wakefield; cauliflower Snow Crown; lettuce Black Seeded Simpson, Red Sails, Buttercrunch; spinach Melody, Tyee; kale Dwarf Blue Curled; collards Blue Max, Georgia; carrots Royal Chantenay (short, good in clay), Danvers 126.

🧅 Onions

Day length decides everything. Onions start forming bulbs once days reach a set length. Grow short-day varieties, which bulb at 10–12 hours of daylight. Intermediate-day types like Candy are a reasonable second choice at your latitude. Skip long-day onions (the kind sold up north); they won't bulb properly here.

Varieties: Texas Supersweet 1015Y, Yellow, Red and White Granex, Early Grano 502, Texas Early White, Burgundy. Intermediate-day: Candy.

Planting: set transplants late January to mid-February (earlier is better), ¾ inch deep and 3 inches apart. Or sow seed in October–December; the "1015" is named for sowing around October 15.

Every leaf becomes a ring. The more leaves a plant grows before bulbing starts in spring, the bigger the onion. Feed at planting (2–3 lb of 10-10-10 per 100 sq ft) and again at the 5–6 leaf stage (½ cup per 10 ft of row).

Harvest May–July, when the necks soften and the tops fall over. Let them dry in the garden 1–2 days, then cure in shade. Sweet onions keep about 3 months; see the Root Cellar tab for storage.

🧄 Garlic

Planted in fall, harvested the next summer. Plant cloves late September through October (AgriLife's fall guide lists October for your region), 1–2 inches deep, pointed end up, 3–6 inches apart. Wait until the soil a couple of inches down is below 85°F.

Softneck types are the safe bet in Texas: California Early, California White, Creole types, Lorz Italian, Inchelium Red, Texas Rose or Texas White. Elephant garlic (really a leek) does well too. Hardneck types prefer colder winters; yours are colder than Central Texas, so a small trial is fine, but it's unproven here.

Harvest roughly late May to June, once the lower ⅓–½ of the leaves have browned. Cure by hanging in shade with good airflow for 2–4 weeks, then trim roots to ¼ inch and tops to about 2 inches.

🥔 Irish potatoes

Plant when soil 4 inches down reaches about 50°F, 4–6 weeks before last frost: late February to early March for you. (The Texas tradition is around Valentine's or Presidents' Day; your later frost date nudges it a little later.)

Seed pieces: 5–6 days before planting, cut seed potatoes into 1½–2 oz pieces (about the size of a hen's egg), each with at least one good eye. Let them cure in a ventilated spot so the cut sides seal over. Plant 3–4 inches deep, 10–12 inches apart, rows 30–36 inches apart.

Growing: pull soil up around the stems as they grow (hilling), and mulch thickly once plants are 8–10 inches tall. Keep moisture even; drought followed by heavy watering gives cracked, knobby tubers.

Varieties: Red LaSoda, Dark Red Norland, Viking, Kennebec, Yukon Gold, Russet Norkotah.

Harvest 75–110 days after planting, once the tops die back and the skins are firm. A fall crop is possible but marginal: small whole potatoes planted in mid-July into hot soil. AgriLife's sources don't agree on timing, so treat it as an experiment.

🍠 Sweet potatoes

Grown from slips (rooted vine cuttings), not seed. Plant once the soil is above 65°F, at least 2 weeks after last frost: late April through May for you. One of the best crops for your summers; it loves heat.

Planting: 3–4 inches deep, 12–24 inches apart, on ridges about 8 inches high with rows 3–4 ft apart. Give about an inch of water a week, and stop watering 2–3 weeks before harvest.

Varieties: Beauregard (most popular), Centennial, Jewel, Vardaman (compact, about 100 days).

Harvest after 100–130 days, before the soil cools below about 55°F, which causes chilling injury.

Cure them, or they won't taste right. Hold at 80–85°F and 85–90% humidity for 7–10 days (a warm room with the roots in loosely closed boxes or bags works). Curing heals nicks so they store without rotting, and turns starch into sugar. Then store at 55–60°F for 3–7 months. Never refrigerate: below 50°F they get hard cores and off flavors.

🌞 Okra & southern peas: your summer workhorses

Both thrive in the heat that shuts tomatoes down, so they fill the garden through July and August.

Okra: wait for soil around 75°F (early to mid-May for you) and soak seed overnight. Plant ½–1 inch deep and thin to 12–24 inches apart. Varieties: Clemson Spineless, Emerald, Lee, Cajun Delight; Blondy and Annie Oakley stay compact; Burgundy is red. Pick pods at 2–4 inches every 1–2 days. They turn woody fast, and the plant slows down if pods are left on. A second planting needs at least 70–90 days before frost (by early to mid-August).

Southern peas (black-eyed, purple hull, cream, crowder): plant 2–10 weeks after last frost, or 10–12 weeks before first frost for a fall crop. Varieties: Texas Pink Eye, Texas Cream 8, Zipper Cream, California #5 (black-eyed), Mississippi Silver.

🌿 Herbs

Cool-season (fall, winter, early spring): cilantro, parsley and dill. Cilantro, a 2026 Texas Superstar, loves 60° days and bolts at 90°. It's ready in 35–45 days, takes light frost, and reseeds if you let a few plants flower. Sow a little every few weeks.

Warm-season: basil. Plant after frost; it dies at the first freeze. Sweet and Spicy Globe are on AgriLife's North Central Texas list.

Perennials: rosemary, oregano (including Mexican oregano), thyme and sage come back every year but need sharp drainage. In clay, give them a raised bed; sage especially hates wet clay.

Mint: keep it in a pot. It spreads underground and will take over a bed.

🌿 Asparagus — a perennial bed, planned once

Unlike everything else on this page, asparagus is a perennial — plant it once and, if the bed is sized and sited well, it keeps producing for 15–20+ years. That also means it's worth getting the planning right up front rather than starting small and expanding later, since you won't want to disturb an established bed.

Good news for your spot: North and West Texas (which covers Callahan County) is actually better suited to asparagus than most of the rest of the state. It needs real winter cold to go properly dormant, and struggles more where summers are long and hot and winters stay mild — exactly the opposite of your climate.

Sizing for a real harvest (not a token patch): AgriLife suggests 10–15 plants per person, so plan on roughly 20–30 crowns for your family, spaced about 18 inches apart. A bed about 3–4 ft wide with two staggered rows, 12–20 ft long, fits that comfortably.

Soil & drainage: asparagus wants deep, well-drained soil — the single biggest way it fails is crown rot from sitting wet, a real risk in this area's clay. Build a raised bed with at least 12–18 inches of good, compost-amended soil over loosened native ground.

Varieties: Mary Washington, Martha Washington, UC 157, or Jersey Giant all do well in Texas.

Planting: set crowns in late winter. AgriLife's table puts it 4–6 weeks before your last frost, about late February to early March. Plant 6–8 inches deep in a trench using the mound-and-backfill method: set each crown on a small mound at the trench bottom, spread the roots down the sides, and fill the trench in gradually as shoots grow through the season.

Patience required: don't harvest at all the first year, harvest lightly the second, and let the bed reach full production by year three. After that, it's one of the lowest-effort things in the garden for well over a decade.

🟢 naturally low histamine Asparagus is eaten fresh rather than fermented, cured, or aged — the preparations that tend to raise histamine levels — so it's one of the better vegetable options for Riley's diet. It's also naturally gluten-free with nothing to swap or watch for.

🍓 Strawberries

Texas grows them as a fall-planted annual. Plant in October, harvest March–May, then pull the plants. AgriLife calls this the best system for Texas; the northern "matted row" method isn't recommended here. Order plants early, since they ship in late September.

Layout: raised beds 2½–3 ft apart, two rows per bed 18 inches apart, plants 12 inches apart.

Varieties: Chandler (most common and well adapted), Sweet Charlie, Festival, Camarosa, Camino Real.

Care: cover with row cover on freezing nights once they bloom. Pick when the berries are at least 75% red. They tolerate soil up to about pH 7.6.

🫐 Blackberries

One of the most productive fruits for a homestead: first crop in year 2, then 5–10 lb per plant.

Varieties: thornless Natchez, Ouachita, Arapaho, Apache. Thorny types produce more: Brazos (earliest), Kiowa (largest berries), Rosborough, Chickasaw. Primocane types (Prime-Ark 45, Prime-Jim, Prime-Jan) fruit on first-year canes.

Soil: they struggle on alkaline soil and show iron chlorosis (yellow new leaves). Amend heavily or use raised beds, and keep chelated iron on hand.

Planting: mid-to-late winter, 2–3 ft apart in rows 8–12 ft apart. Trellis on two wires at 30 and 60 inches.

Pruning: each cane dies after it fruits. Cut fruited canes to the ground right after harvest, and tip new canes in summer so they branch.

🫐 Blueberries — containers only here

Blueberries need soil at pH 4.0–5.5. They won't thrive in your alkaline ground, and AgriLife says trying to acidify native soil usually fails. Alkaline well water is a second problem, since its calcium bicarbonate and sodium hurt them.

The workaround AgriLife describes: 20–30 gallon containers of a peat moss and pine bark mix, watered with rainwater or air-conditioner condensate. It works, but it's ongoing effort, and keeping enough rainwater through a dry summer is the hard part.

Varieties: rabbiteye types only — Tifblue, Climax, Premier, Brightwell, Powderblue, Austin. Plant two varieties for better fruit set.

🍇 Grapes

Pierce's disease is the big risk. It's a bacterial disease spread by sharpshooter insects, and there was an outbreak across the West Cross Timbers (your neighborhood) in the late 1990s. European wine grapes are high-risk here.

Pierce's-tolerant grapes: Black Spanish (Lenoir) grows across Texas but watch for iron chlorosis; Blanc du Bois (white); Victoria Red (table grape); Champanel (handles heavy clay, great for jelly); Herbemont; Lomanto.

Muscadines are a poor fit; they want pH around 6 and yellow on alkaline soil.

Pruning: February–March, to a single cordon with spurs of 2–3 buds.

🍑 Fruit trees & chill hours

Chill hours are the hours at or below 45°F a tree gets over winter. A variety needs about its rated number to bloom and fruit properly. Too few chill hours and buds don't develop; a low-chill variety blooms too early and gets frosted. Your area is estimated at around 800 chill hours (AgriLife's North Central Texas list is built on 800–900) — confirm with the Callahan County Extension agent before you buy. Pick varieties within about 150 hours of your number.

Peaches (650–850 hour types): Junegold, Juneprince, Flavorich, Regal, Cary Mac, Dixiland, Redskin, Harvester, Sentinel, Majestic, Redglobe, Cresthaven, Fireprince. Expect to lose about one crop in six or seven to late frost. First crop in year 3–4; prune to an open center every winter.

Plums: Methley (self-fruitful and pollinates others), Bruce (needs Methley nearby), Morris (needs a pollinator and 800+ chill hours). Most plums need two varieties.

Pears: West Texas has the lowest fire blight pressure in the state. Kieffer, Moonglow, Orient, Warren, Ayers, Magness, Maxine, LeConte. Asian pears bear around year 4. On heavy, high-pH soil use Pyrus betulifolia rootstock.

Apples are harder here since they prefer slightly acidic soil. For your chill level: Gala/Royal Gala, Fuji, Mollie's Delicious, Mutsu, Pink Lady on MM111 or EM7 rootstock. Plant two varieties for pollination.

Cherries are a poor bet. AgriLife says sweet cherries have performed poorly in Texas (high chill needs, brown rot, birds). Montmorency tart cherry is only worth a trial.

Cotton root rot is a soil fungus common in alkaline clay across this area. It kills fruit and nut trees suddenly (pears are very susceptible) and there's no effective fungicide. Avoid spots where trees or shrubs have died suddenly before.

When to plant: bare-root trees December through early March while dormant; container trees can go in most of the year. On high-pH soil AgriLife suggests nitrogen-only fertilizer plus chelated iron and zinc as needed.

🟤 Figs

Varieties: Celeste is the most cold-hardy and your best bet. Texas Everbearing (sold as Brown Turkey) is less hardy but fruits June through August. Alma is excellent but very frost-sensitive while young.

Cold: figs handle a sustained 17°F and survive the low teens with some damage, so your coldest winters will nip them some years. Before a hard freeze, water deeply; mound hay 2–3 ft up the trunk of older trees, and cage young plants and stuff the cage with hay or leaves. If one freezes to the ground, keep 5–6 strong new shoots as the new trunks.

🌰 Pecans

A long game: expect few nuts for the first 5–7 years, and space trees 50–100 ft apart.

Varieties (AgriLife's West region list): Pawnee, Caddo, Cheyenne, Lipan, Waco, Western (Type I) and Hopi, Lakota, Wichita (Type II). Plant both types so they pollinate each other; about 15% pollinator trees is enough. Callahan County sits near the edge of AgriLife's West, North and Central regions.

Zinc is essential on alkaline soil. Spray zinc sulfate at 2 teaspoons per gallon: every 2 weeks April to early August on young trees, and at least 3 times between budbreak and early June on mature trees. West-region trees also need irrigation for a good crop.

🚫 What won't thrive here (and workarounds)

Rhubarb: AgriLife says it doesn't grow well anywhere in Texas as a perennial; summer heat and crown rot kill it. Grow it as a cool-season annual instead: start Victoria seed indoors in mid-to-late August, transplant late September to early October, and harvest March–May. Eat only the stalks; the leaves are toxic.

Mango: leaves are damaged below 30°F, so in Texas it only grows outdoors in the Lower Rio Grande Valley. Here it would have to be a container plant brought indoors for winter.

Cherries and blueberries: see Fruit trees and Blueberries above.

🤝 Companion planting: what's real and what's folklore

Companion planting charts online mix real research with garden folklore. Each idea here is labeled so you know which is which: ✅ good evidence 🟡 some evidence ⚪ folklore

The single practice with the strongest evidence isn't a pairing at all — it's crop rotation (bottom of this page).

⚪ Folklore: plant it for fun, not for results

  • Basil makes tomatoes taste better: three years of blind taste tests found no difference.
  • Onions and garlic repel pests: no evidence as a general rule. Any mix of plants confuses some pests a little; it isn't special to onions.
  • "Carrots love tomatoes" and big companion charts: one horticulturist compares them to horoscopes.
  • Onions stunt beans and peas: no study or mechanism found.
  • Nasturtiums as an aphid trap: they attract aphids, but no field study shows they protect a crop.
  • Fennel poisons its neighbors: weak evidence; shade and water competition are the likelier explanation.

🚧 Real reasons to keep things apart

  • Black walnut: its roots release juglone, which harms tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, eggplant and asparagus up to 50–60 ft from a mature tree. Black walnut isn't common in your area; pecans are relatives but produce far less of it. ✅ good
  • Tomatoes after potatoes (or any nightshade after another): they share early and late blight. Side by side is fine; following one with the other in the same bed is the problem. ✅ good
  • Sunflower hulls: sunflower residue suppresses seedlings, so don't let seed hulls pile up in beds (like under a bird feeder). 🟡 some

🔄 Crop rotation: the one with the strongest evidence

Don't grow the same plant family in the same spot more than once every 3–4 years. Related crops share soil diseases and pests, and rotation starves them out. It helps less against insects that fly in (squash bugs, leaffooted bugs), and root-knot nematodes eat so many crops that rotation alone won't stop them; add nematode-resistant (VFN) tomatoes and a marigold cover crop.

🐛 What AgriLife recommends for two Texas pests

Leaffooted bugs (tomatoes, fruit): scout often, hand-pick or vacuum them off, and clear tall grass and weeds near the garden.

Harlequin bugs (brassicas): hand-pick adults, crush the black-and-white barrel-shaped egg clusters, and use insecticidal soap, neem or pyrethrin on the young nymphs.

Seed starting basics

  • Depth: as a rule of thumb, plant a seed about twice as deep as it is wide. Very fine seed (lettuce, some herbs) often needs light to germinate — press into the surface instead of burying.
  • Light: a sunny south-facing window is rarely bright enough for stocky seedlings; a shop light or grow light 2–3 inches above the seedlings, on 14–16 hrs/day, prevents leggy stretching.
  • Temperature: most seeds germinate fastest around 65–75°F; a seedling heat mat helps in a cool room, especially for peppers and tomatoes.
  • Hardening off: over 7–10 days before transplanting outside, gradually increase seedlings' outdoor time and direct sun exposure so they don't scorch or wilt from the shock of wind, sun, and temperature swings.

When in doubt, throw it out

Signs a preserved food should be discarded without tasting: a bulging or leaking lid, a jar that spurts liquid or foam when opened, cloudy liquid in a food that's normally clear, mold of any color, a foul or "off" smell, or a seal that failed. For low-acid home-canned food specifically, if you have any doubt about whether it was processed correctly, the CDC guidance is to boil it hard for 10 minutes (plus 1 minute per 1,000 ft of elevation) before even smelling or tasting it — or simply discard it. Never taste suspect food to test it; botulism toxin can be present without any noticeable change in smell or taste.

Highest botulism-risk foods

Low-acid, home-canned or vacuum-sealed foods that were water-bath canned instead of pressure canned, or under-processed: green beans, corn, beets, asparagus, plain vegetables in general, meat, poultry, seafood, and any low-acid food stored in oil at room temperature (like garlic-in-oil). Commercially canned goods carry far lower risk because they're processed under strict industrial controls.

🔴 Low-histamine basics for a homestead kitchen

Histamine isn't something you add — it builds up as bacteria and enzymes break food down over time. That's why the same processes that make food shelf-stable (fermenting, curing, aging, slow drying) are exactly the ones that raise histamine the most. A few rules of thumb that show up throughout this guide:

  • Fresh and freshly frozen is lowest-risk. Freezing at 0°F essentially halts further histamine buildup — but it doesn't reverse histamine that's already formed, so the timing matters: freeze meat, fish, and cooked leftovers within 1–2 hours of cooking or harvest, not after they've sat out or spent days in the fridge.
  • Refrigerated leftovers still climb. High-protein leftovers (meat, fish, poultry, bone broth) measurably gain histamine over 24–48 hours in the fridge, even though they're perfectly food-safe. For a low-histamine eater, that's the practical reason to serve/freeze leftovers within a day rather than eating them all week.
  • Fermented, cured, aged, and smoked foods are inherently high-histamine — the bacteria doing the preserving work is the same bacteria producing histamine as a byproduct. This is true no matter how carefully or safely it's made.
  • A few fresh foods are commonly flagged too — independent of preservation method, tomatoes, spinach, eggplant, citrus, strawberries, pineapple, and vinegar itself show up on most low-histamine avoid-lists. Something can be "safe" by every food-safety measure in this guide and still be worth watching for a histamine-sensitive kiddo.

None of this means skipping fermenting, curing, or dehydrating for the family — just treat those batches as "not for the low-histamine plate," the same way you'd set aside a separate, smaller portion before it's fermented/cured/smoked for anyone in the house who needs it.

🌾 Gluten-free basics for a homestead kitchen

Almost nothing in canning, curing, fermenting, or dehydrating starts out containing gluten — vegetables, fruit, meat, and salt are all naturally gluten-free. The risk is almost always one specific hidden ingredient in a recipe, not the method itself. The repeat offenders, with swaps:
